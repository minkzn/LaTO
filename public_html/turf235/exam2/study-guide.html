M10

Assigned text: Chapter 3, pp. 66–68
Overview objectives: In this Module you will review information on Ryegrasses. · Identify ryegrasses by their morphological features. · Recognize the conditions to which these turfgrasses are adapted and potential uses based on those adaptations. · Asses and define the cultural requirements of these turfgrasses.

Overview

  • Perennial ryegrass is used to overseed the principally Kentucky bluegrass turf at Beaver Stadium whenever there is damage from play.
  • The rapid germination, vigorous seedling growth and excellent wear tolerance of ryegrass make it almost ideal for this overseeding purpose.
Source: M10.1_ Overview_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Perennial Ryegrass

  • Latin name: Lolium perenne L.
  • Ligule: medium, rounded, membranous; more precisely, membranous, 0.5 to 2 mm long, truncate to rounded.
  • Mowing: 0.5 - 2.0 inches. Fertilization: 2.0 - 6.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: as needed. Overseeding as needed.
  • Golf Turf — Greens: overseeding bermudagrass. Tees: adapted. Fairways: adapted. Roughs: adapted.
  • Athletic: well adapted. Lawn Turf: adapted for moderate-to-high intensity of culture. Utility: not well adapted.
Source: M10.2_ Perennial Ryegrass_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Close-up of Perennial Ryegrass

  • Abaxial leaf surface: glossy, with a strong keel.
  • Turf-type vs. forage-type: turf-type improvements include finer texture, higher density, improved mowing quality, improved stress tolerance, slower shoot growth rate, and more pleasing color.
  • Popularity of perennial ryegrass fairways has diminished due to susceptibility to gray leaf spot disease, which can devastate a fairway in just a few days; seedlings are especially susceptible.
  • On an overseeded bermudagrass green, the dormant winter bermudagrass is covered by an overwintering perennial ryegrass turf that transitions back to bermudagrass the following spring.
  • A fungal endophyte in the seed forms a symbiotic association with the plant, imparting both stress tolerance and foliar-feeding insect resistance; turf without the endophyte is often mostly brown.
Source: M10.3_ Close-up of Perennial Ryegrass_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Annual Ryegrass

  • Latin name: Lolium multiflorum Lam.
  • Ligule: short, rounded, membranous; more precisely, membranous, 0.5 to 2 mm long, rounded.
  • Mowing: 0.5 - 2.0 inches (same as perennial ryegrass). Fertilization: 2.0 - 6.0 lb. N / K ft² / year (same as perennial ryegrass).
  • Golf Turf — Greens and Fairways: sometimes used for overseeding bermudagrass, but the perennial species is preferred. Tees and Roughs: not suitable.
  • Athletic: not suited. Lawn Turf: sometimes used for "repairing" damaged turf, but may persist as objectionable clumps. Utility: not suitable.
  • Because of its intolerance of environmental stresses, annual ryegrass does not form a very good turf for very long.
Source: M10.4_ Annual Ryegrass_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Textbook enrichment — Ch. 3, pp. 66–68

  • The ryegrasses (genus Lolium) include about 10 species distributed primarily within the temperate climatic zone; the two used as turfgrasses are perennial (English) ryegrass and annual (Italian) ryegrass. Traditionally used as nursegrasses in seed mixtures for their rapid germination and vigorous seedling growth.
  • Perennial ryegrass: bunch-type, diploid (2n = 2x = 14); vernation folded; auricles small, soft, clawlike; collar broad, divided; blade 2–5 mm wide; inflorescence a flat spike with awnless spikelets. Grows best in neutral to slightly acid, moist soils of moderate to high fertility. Mowing quality sometimes poor due to tough vascular bundles in the leaf blades. Endophyte: Neotyphodium lolii (formerly Acremonium).
  • Annual ryegrass: bunch-type, diploid (2n = 2x = 14); vernation rolled; auricles pointed, clawlike; collar broad, continuous; blade 3–7 mm wide; abaxial leaf surface smooth and glossy (no keel); inflorescence a flat spike with awned spikelets. Its aggressive seedling growth discourages its use in seed mixtures with more desirable cool-season turfgrasses. Cultivar Panterra was bred for better spring transition.
  • Intermediate ryegrass (Lolium x hybridum Hausskn.) is a hybrid of perennial and annual ryegrass, combining perennial's turf quality with annual's rapid germination and quick spring transition; cultivars include Froghair II, Transeze, and Transist.
  • Improved turf-type perennial ryegrass cultivars include Citation, Derby, Diplomat, Manhattan, Manhattan II, Palmer, Pennant, Pennfine, and Repell.
Source: Turfgrass Management Edition 1.0, Chapter 3, pp. 66–68

M11

Assigned text: Chapter 3, pp. 61–64
Overview objectives: The purpose of this Module is to review Creeping red fescue, Hard fescue, and Tall fescue turfgrasses. · Identify Creeping red fescue, Hard fescue, and Tall fescue turfgrasses based on their morphological features. · Recognize the conditions to which these turfgrasses are adapted and potential uses based on those adaptations. · Assess and define the cultural requirements of these turfgrasses.

Overview

  • The fescues represent the textural extremes of the pooid turfgrasses: fine fescue leaf blades are very fine, almost needlelike, while tall fescue is quite coarse.
  • Tall fescue is seen in large expanses in the Carolinas and other cool subtropical-humid climates; fine fescues are used extensively, especially on shaded sites, in temperate and subarctic climates.
  • Creeping red fescue, hard fescue, and tall fescue — the three species covered in this module — all belong to a single genus: Festuca.
Source: M11.1_ Overview_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Creeping Red Fescue

  • Latin name: Festuca rubra L.
  • Ligule: membranous, 0.1 to 0.4 mm long, truncate.
  • Mowing: 0.75 - 2.5 inches. Fertilization: 2.0 - 6.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: as needed to sustain growth and color. Other: cultivation and practices to control thatch, compaction, and disease.
  • Golf Turf — Greens: well-adapted for certain regions. Tees: marginally adapted. Fairways: marginally adapted for closely mowed fairways. Roughs: well adapted.
  • Athletic: Not well adapted. Lawn Turf: well adapted for low intensities of culture. Utility: well adapted, but other alternatives are available.
Source: M11.2_ Creeping Red Fescue_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Red and Fine Fescues

  • Fine fescue leaf texture: needle-like.
  • Red-Fescue (F. rubra) Complex: strong creeping red fescue (F. rubra ssp. rubra Gaud.), slender creeping red fescue (F. rubra ssp. litoralis [Meyer] Auquier), Chewings fescue (F. rubra ssp. comutata [Thuill.] Nyman).
  • Sheep-Fescue (F. ovina) Complex: sheep fescue (F. ovina ssp. hirtula [Hackel ex Travis] Wilkinson), hard fescue (F. trachyphylla [Hackel] Krajina).
  • Table 11.3.1 — Strong creeping red fescue: 56 chromosomes, forms a more open turf suitable for mixtures with Kentucky bluegrass. Slender creeping red fescue: 42 chromosomes, denser turf, suitable alone or in blends. Chewings fescue: 42 chromosomes.
  • Plants within the red fescue complex have five distinct ridges on the adaxial leaf blade.
  • Fine fescues are drought tolerant, do well in shaded environments, and are popular for shaded/low-maintenance lawns and unmowed (and some mowed) golf course roughs. Red fescues can be very thatch prone, especially in sunny environments where growth is vigorous.
Source: M11.3_ Red and Fine Fescues_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Hard Fescue

  • Latin name (per module page): Festuca longifolia Thiull.
  • Ligule: membranous, 0.3 mm long, rounded.
  • Mowing: 1.5 - 2.5 inches. Fertilization: 1.0 - 2.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: not needed. Other: some thatch control may be needed.
  • Golf Turf — Greens: overseeding bermuda grass. Tees and Fairways: not suitable. Roughs: well adapted.
  • Athletic: not suitable. Lawn Turf: adaptable to a low-to-moderate intensity of culture. Utility: adaptable.
  • Table 11.4.1 — Sheep fescue: 56 chromosomes (per this module page's table), forms a blueish-green turf of limited utility. Hard fescue: 42 chromosomes, denser turf with exceptional drought tolerance.
  • Well suited for use near sand bunkers because of its heat and drought tolerance; like red fescues, works well in dry, infertile shaded environments.
Source: M11.4_ Hard Fescue_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Tall Fescue

  • Latin name (per module page): Schedonorus phoenix [Scop.] Holub.
  • Ligule: membranous, 0.4 to 1.2 mm long, truncate.
  • Mowing: > 1.5 inches. Fertilization: 1.0 - 5.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: moderate or as needed. Other: fall overseeding as needed; diseases such as brown patch and grey leaf spot.
  • Golf Turf — Greens and Tees: not suitable. Fairways: marginally adapted. Roughs: adapted.
  • Athletic: marginally adapted. Lawn Turf: marginally adapted. Utility: well adapted — an excellent utility turf, including on racing tracks; a popular lawn grass, especially in "transition-zone" climates.
  • Intergeneric hybridization: perennial ryegrass × tall fescue produces Festulolium; backcrossed with perennial ryegrass it's identified as BC1PR, backcrossed with tall fescue as BC1TF.
  • Because of the absence of lateral shoots, netting is typically used in tall fescue sod production to hold the sod together. Endophyte-free vs. endophyte-enhanced comparisons show improved drought and insect tolerance; the effect on disease resistance varies with the disease. In a drought demonstration, potted plants given a drought-tolerance gene fared much better after six days without water than normal plants.
Source: M11.5_ Tall Fescue_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Textbook enrichment — Ch. 3, pp. 61–64

  • Pooids (formerly Festucoids) are cool-season, C3 grasses within fourteen tribes, three of which (Festuceae, Aveneae, Triticeae) include turfgrass genera; the Poeae tribe's four major genera are Festuca, Poa, Lolium, and Schedonorus.
  • Of roughly 100 Festuca species, only a few are used as turfgrasses, split into the red-fescue (F. rubra) and sheep-fescue (F. ovina) complexes. Sheep-fescue complex leaves are finer than red-fescue complex leaves. Red-fescue complex sheaths are fused almost to the top with extravaginal tillering; sheep-fescue complex sheaths are at least 40% free with intravaginal tillering.
  • Strong creeping red fescue: octoploid (56 chromosomes); well-drained, moderately shaded, droughty infertile acid soils; intolerant of wet conditions/high fertility; used with Kentucky bluegrass or colonial bentgrass, or with perennial ryegrass for winter overseeding. Cultivars: Aberdeen, Arctared, Boreal, Ruby, Wintergreen, SR 5120, and others.
  • Slender creeping red fescue: hexaploid (42 chromosomes); cultivars Dawson (and dwarfs Logro, Elfin, Count) and Seabreeze (a hybrid with Chewings fescue). Chewings fescue: hexaploid (42 chromosomes); forms a denser turf under the close mowing practiced in northwestern Europe; somewhat less tolerant of temperature extremes than creeping red fescue. Cultivars: Fairmont, Jamestown II, Victory, and others.
  • Sheep fescue (textbook): tetraploid (28 chromosomes — note this differs from the 56 shown on the M11.4 module page's own table); used for soil stabilization; very low cultural intensity. Hard fescue (textbook): hexaploid (42 chromosomes), tougher/wider leaves with five to seven ribs; less drought tolerant but more tolerant of moist, fertile soils than sheep fescue. Cultivars: Aurora, Biljart, Scaldis, and others.
  • Tall fescue (Schedonorus arundinaceus): hexaploid (42 chromosomes); good heat/drought tolerance for a cool-season grass, but relatively poor cold tolerance. Cultivars: forage types Alta and Kentucky-31; turf types Rebel (the first), Falcon, Olympic; dwarf types Trailblazer (the first), Bonsai, Matador; intermediate types Millennium, Plantation, Rembrandt. Endophyte: Neotyphodium coenophialum.
  • Meadow fescue (Schedonorus pratensis): similar to tall fescue but more widely used in Europe; diploid (14 chromosomes); no improved turf-type cultivars currently available.
  • Endophytes (Neotyphodium typhinum and Epichloe typhina, formerly Acremonium) included in creeping red, slender creeping red, Chewings, and hard fescue cultivars improve drought resistance, insect tolerance, and resistance to some diseases.
  • The "transition zone" is the cool subtropical-humid climatic type; tall fescue and some zoysiagrasses are uniquely adapted to it. Colonial bentgrass and fine fescues are well adapted to the temperate oceanic climate of northern Europe and the U.S. Pacific Northwest east of the Cascades.
Source: Turfgrass Management Edition 1.0, Chapter 3, pp. 61–64

M12

Assigned text: Turfgrass Management, 9th Edition, Chapter 3 (endophyte references within the species descriptions)
Overview objectives: Define and describe an endophyte. · Identify which turfgrasses have endophytes. · Determine how endophytes impart pest and drought resistance to "infected" turfgrasses. · Recognize diseases associated with the presence of endophytes. · Show how the presence of an endophyte is detected.

Overview

  • Most fungi are beneficial "decomposers" that break down organic residues. An endophyte is a beneficial fungus whose symbiotic association with a host plant imparts greater drought, insect, and disease resistance than would otherwise occur — raising the prospect of pest control without pesticides.
Source: M12.1_ Overview_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

The Good Fungi

  • In side-by-side fine fescue plots, the endophyte-free plot was heavily infested with dollar spot disease while the endophyte-containing plot was disease-free. The fungus is visible microscopically inside leaf-sheath cells; grown in petri dishes on agar medium, colonies show variation. Endophyte-containing plots resisted the normal decline seen under mid-summer stress.
  • Gwinn et al. (1991): an endophyte lives its life cycle within a host plant without causing disease — the definition doesn't specify fungi, since bacterial endophytes occur in bermudagrasses (no benefits yet observed from those).
  • Table 12.2.1 — Perennial Ryegrass: Neotyphodium lolii. Tall Fescue: Neotyphodium conophialum. Fine Fescue: Neotyphodium typhinum and Epichloe typhina. Currently, only the ryegrasses and fescues contain fungal endophytes.
  • Endophytes have been a problem in forage grasses because of alkaloid poisons (permine — poisonous to insects — plus Lolitrem B, Paxilline, Ergovaline, and others) that adversely affect grazing herbivores; these are translocated throughout the plant.
  • In turfgrasses, secondary metabolites confer resistance to sod webworms, billbugs, white grubs, fall army worms, and chinch bugs; endophyte infection confers dollar spot resistance but greater Pythium susceptibility, and improved drought tolerance (scientific basis unknown).
Source: M12.2_ The Good Fungi_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Endophytes

  • Breeders are trying to incorporate fungal endophytes into bentgrasses and bluegrasses, though success is unproven since the symbiosis is believed to result from a long evolutionary process.
  • Endophytes occur in three locations: the crown tissue, the flowering culm, and the seed. The crown is a highly compressed stem at the base of aerial shoots; when it shifts from vegetative to reproductive growth, the endophyte travels up with the developing inflorescence, leaving traces in the flowering culm.
  • In fine fescues, the endophyte sometimes causes "choke" disease, named for choking off further development of the inflorescence.
  • Detection methods: the seed-squash technique (seeds soaked, swelled, stained, and squashed on a slide to view hyphae in the aleurone layer just inside the seed coat) and the grow-out test (microscopically examining seedlings for the percentage containing the endophyte, yielding percent viability). The endophyte is initially restricted to the aleurone layer; during germination it infects the embryo and seedling. Because it cannot produce reproductive spores, it is transmitted only through the seed.
  • Benefits: improved root growth, seedling vigor, competitive ability, heat and drought tolerance, and pest resistance. The seed industry markets "endophyte enhanced" seed.
  • Viability depends on seed storage: store below 5°C and below 11% moisture. Tall fescue and perennial ryegrass seed stored over two years has probably lost much endophyte viability; in fine fescues, viability may not last beyond one year.
Source: M12.3_ Endophytes_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Textbook enrichment — Ch. 3

  • Each fescue and ryegrass cultivar group's endophyte is named in the textbook's species descriptions, every one formerly classified as Acremonium: creeping red fescue, slender creeping red fescue, Chewings fescue, and hard fescue cultivars carry Neotyphodium typhinum and Epichloe typhina; tall fescue cultivars carry Neotyphodium coenophialum; perennial ryegrass cultivars carry Neotyphodium lolii. In every case, endophyte inclusion is credited with improving drought resistance, insect tolerance, and resistance to some diseases.
Source: Turfgrass Management Edition 1.0, Chapter 3

M13

Assigned text: Chapter 3, pp. 68–70
Overview objectives: The purpose of this Module is to review Creeping bentgrass, Colonial bentgrass, Velvet bentgrass, and Redtop turfgrasses. · Identify Creeping bentgrass, Colonial bentgrass, Velvet bentgrass, and Redtop turfgrasses based on their morphological features. · Recognize the conditions to which these turfgrasses are adapted and potential uses based on those adaptations. · Assess and define the cultural requirements of these turfgrasses.

Overview

  • Western Oregon's climate features mild, wet winters and cool, dry summers. The Oregon Golf Club example: Pennlinks creeping bentgrass greens, Penncross creeping bentgrass tees, Bardot colonial bentgrass fairways.
  • Velvet bentgrass can be found in more than just little patches on old greens in Rhode Island. Even though little-used today, redtop can persist on wet, poorly drained, trafficked sites where no other turfgrass will.
Source: M13.1_ Overview_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Creeping Bentgrass

  • Latin name: Agrostis stolonifera L. (formerly A. palustris Huds.).
  • Ligule: long, rounded, membranous; more precisely, membranous, 0.6 to 3 mm long, finely toothed or entire, rounded.
  • Mowing: 0.12 - 0.5 inches. Fertilization: 2.0 - 6.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: needed frequently. Other: many practices required — cultivation, topdressing, pest (disease) control.
  • Golf Turf — Greens, Tees, Fairways: well adapted. Roughs: not suitable.
  • Athletic: not suitable. Lawn Turf: adapted for intensively cultured lawns only. Utility: not suitable.
Source: M13.2_ Creeping Bentgrass_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Creeping Bentgrass — Cultivar History

  • At lawn height, creeping bentgrass forms ugly patches in the turf; it must be mowed closer to sustain acceptable quality.
  • Table 13.3.2 (quality rankings by region): L-93 ranked #1 USA overall; Seaside ranked last (28th). The Penn A- and G-series underperformed in Georgia's acid soils. Crenshaw ranked poorly in PA/NJ (22) due to dollar spot susceptibility, but ranked 2nd in Iowa, where dollar spot is rare. Penn G-2 ranked #1 in Iowa; Seaside's best regional rank (2) was in Georgia.
Source: M13.3_ Creeping Bentgrass_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Colonial Bentgrass

  • Latin name: Agrostis capillaris L.
  • Ligule: short, truncate, membranous; more precisely, membranous, 0.3 to 1.2 mm long, truncate.
  • Mowing: 0.3 - 0.8 inches. Fertilization: 1.0 - 2.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: needed moderately. Other: varies with mowing height; may require topdressing.
  • Golf Turf — Greens, Tees, Fairways, Roughs: all adapted.
  • Athletic: not well adapted. Lawn Turf: adapted to low intensities of culture. Utility: marginally adapted.
  • Winter: a snow mold susceptibility differential exists between creeping and colonial bentgrass. Summer: the pattern reverses — colonial bentgrass is highly susceptible to brown patch, which limits its use mainly to temperate oceanic climates with milder summers; genetic resistance to brown patch could dramatically expand its use.
  • Forms excellent turf on croquet courts; popular on tennis courts in the UK and other temperate-oceanic climates; example lawn turf shown in coastal Rhode Island.
Source: M13.4_ Colonial Bentgrass_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Velvet Bentgrass

  • Latin name: Agrostis canina L.
  • Ligule: very long, pointed, membranous. (A ligule generally is the membranous or hairy appendage on a grass leaf's adaxial side, at the junction of leaf and blade.)
  • Mowing: 0.2 - 0.4 inches. Fertilization: 1.0 - 2.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: low to moderate. Other: requires many practices, especially topdressing.
  • Golf Turf — Greens, Fairways, Roughs: adapted. Tees: not suitable.
  • Athletic: not adaptable. Lawn Turf: adaptable for low to moderate intensity of culture. Utility: marginally adapted.
  • Especially fine-textured; a serious thatch former requiring frequent topdressing; slow growth means aggressive cultivation must be avoided, and recuperative capacity is very slow. Most evident as dense patches in old greens established with a mixture of bentgrass species.
Source: M13.5_ Velvet Bentgrass_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Redtop

  • Latin name (per module page): Agrostis gigantea With.
  • Ligule: long, rounded, membranous; more precisely, membranous, 1.5 to 5 mm long, rounded — the longest ligule among this module's bentgrasses.
  • Mowing: 1.5 - 2.0 inches (far taller than the other bentgrasses here). Fertilization: 1.0 - 2.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: low to moderate. Other: not required.
  • Golf Turf — Greens, Tees, Fairways, Roughs: all not adapted.
  • Athletic: not adaptable. Lawn Turf: not adaptable. Utility: can be used in poorly drained sites.
Source: M13.6_ Redtop_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Textbook enrichment — Ch. 3, pp. 68–70

  • Aveneae tribe: one major genus (Agrostis), one minor genus (Phleum/timothy); ploidy ranges from diploid to decaploid (2n = 10x = 70). Bentgrass (Agrostis L.) includes ~125 species; six are used as turfgrasses: creeping, colonial, dryland, Idaho, velvet bentgrass, and redtop. Except for Idaho bentgrass and redtop, these are used extensively for greens and other intensively cultured turfs. Common features: ridged adaxial leaf blades, rolled vernation, single-floret spikelets.
  • Creeping bentgrass: tetraploid (28 chromosomes); the most widely used cool-season grass for golf greens. Historical South German Mixed Bent greens contained 1-5% creeping bentgrass, 75-85% colonial/dryland, and 10-20% velvet bentgrass; over time these separated into patches of mostly creeping and, to a lesser extent, velvet bentgrass. USGA-selected patches were propagated at Arlington, Virginia (now the Pentagon site) as the vegetatively-propagated C-series cultivars (Arlington, Cohansey, Congressional, etc.), since they couldn't be established true-to-type from seed. The only seeded C-series-era cultivar was Seaside (salt-tolerant, from western Oregon's tidal flats, but inferior quality) until Penncross arrived in 1955 — first-generation seed from randomly crossing three vegetatively propagated clonal strains. Cultivar density classes: low-density/prostrate (Penncross); medium-density/upright (Brighton, Cato, Crenshaw, Pennlinks, SR 1020, etc.); high-density/upright (007, Penn A-/G-series, Pure Distinction, T-1, V8, etc.), tolerating mowing as low as 0.125 inch.
  • Colonial bentgrass: tetraploid (28 chromosomes), vs. dryland bentgrass's hexaploid 42; poor heat/drought tolerance limits it to oceanic climates (US Pacific NW, New England, NW Europe, New Zealand). US cultivars: Alister, Allure, Astoria, Bardot, Duchess, etc.; N. European cultivars Holfior and Boral; common US dryland bentgrass type: Highland.
  • Idaho bentgrass: broader/darker leaves than colonial/dryland, lacks rhizomes and false crowns; favors moist stream-bank conditions; single cultivar GolfStar.
  • Velvet bentgrass: diploid (14 chromosomes) — fewer than any other bentgrass here; more tolerant of shade, drought, and soil acidity than other bentgrasses; reddish, spreading-panicle inflorescence; best current cultivars SR 7200, Greenwich, Vesper.
  • Redtop (Agrostis gigantea Roth. in the textbook, vs. "With." on its own module page; also identified under the alternate name Agrostis alba L.): coarse-textured, grayish green, rhizomatous; hexaploid (42 chromosomes); historically used in seed mixtures for rapid cover, though its competitive seedling growth and unsightly persistence discourage this; intolerant of close mowing (needs ≥1.5-2 inches); sometimes used as a "repair grass," though fine fescues or ryegrasses are preferred; cultivar Rudiger offers improved drought, salinity, and alkalinity tolerance.
Source: Turfgrass Management Edition 1.0, Chapter 3, pp. 68–70

M14

Assigned text: Chapter 3, pp. 74–77, 83
Overview objectives: The purpose of this Module is to review Bermudagrasses, Zoysiagrasses, and Buffalograss turfgrasses. · Identify Bermudagrasses, Zoysiagrasses, and Buffalograss turfgrasses based on their morphological features. · Recognize the conditions to which these turfgrasses are adapted and potential uses based on those adaptations. · Assess and define the cultural requirements of these turfgrasses.

Overview

  • The chloridoid species — bermudagrasses, zoysiagrasses, and buffalograss — are important for sports, lawn, and utility turfs across the southern half of the U.S. and in subtropical/tropical climates worldwide.
  • In Florida's warm-subtropical-humid and tropical climates, hybrid bermudagrass substitutes for the bentgrasses used in colder climates on greens and other golf turfs. In St. Louis' cool-subtropical-humid climate, zoysiagrass is an important fairway species. With new cultivars, buffalograss is becoming popular, especially in dry climates.
Source: M14.1_ Overview_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Bermudagrasses

  • Common bermudagrass (Cynodon dactylon) is a tetraploid with 36 chromosomes; African bermudagrass (Cynodon transvaalensis) is a diploid with 18 chromosomes and is little used directly. Crossing the two produces a sterile hybrid with 27 chromosomes — hybrid bermudagrass — which, occurring naturally, is called Magennis bermudagrass (Cynodon magennisii). Sterile hybrids must be propagated vegetatively.
  • Common Bermudagrass (Cynodon dactylon [L.]) — Ligule: a fringe of hairs, 2–5 mm long. Mowing: 1.0–2.0 inches. Fertilization: 2.0–5.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation and other practices vary with cultural intensity.
  • Golf Turf — Greens: not well adapted. Tees, Fairways, Roughs: adapted. Athletic: well adapted. Lawn: adapted to sunny locations. Utility: adaptable.
Source: M14.2_ Bermudagrasses_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Hybrid Bermudagrass

  • Latin name: (Cynodon dactylon X C. transvaalensis). Ligule: a fringe of hairs, 1–3 mm long; auricles absent.
  • Mowing: 3/16 – 3/4 inches. Fertilization: 3.0–10.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: frequently used. Other: many practices, especially on greens.
  • Golf Turf — Greens, Tees, Fairways: adapted. Roughs: not well adapted. Athletic: well adapted. Lawn: adapted to sunny locations, requires high intensity of culture. Utility: not adaptable.
  • Greens are often overseeded for winter color/play with perennial ryegrass (also annual/intermediate ryegrass, rough bluegrass, fine fescues, creeping bentgrass), protecting dormant bermudagrass from wear injury. Bermudagrass stolons invading bentgrass greens from the collar are blocked with plastic barriers. Prone to scalp in summer when growth is vigorous and thatch forms; topdressing, coring with reincorporation, and vertical mowing control it.
Source: M14.3_ Hybrid Bermudagrass_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Zoysiagrasses

  • Three primary species: Japanese lawngrass (Z. japonica, coarsest), manilagrass (Z. matrella, intermediate), and mascarenegrass (Z. pacifica, finest) — all tetraploids with 40 chromosomes that easily interbreed into interspecific hybrids. Z. pacifica is used as ground cover on slopes and as a hybridization parent. Zoysiagrasses are slow to establish but vary in spread rate by cultivar.
  • Common zoysiagrass (Zoysia spp.) — Ligule: a fringe of hairs. Mowing: 0.5–1.0 inches. Fertilization: 1.5–3.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation as needed; cultivations for thatch control needed.
  • Golf Turf — Greens: not well adapted. Tees: adapted, but poor recuperative capacity. Fairways: well adapted. Roughs: not well adapted. Athletic: not well adapted (poor recuperative capacity). Lawn: adaptable. Utility: not well adapted.

Buffalograss

  • Latin name (module page): Buchloe dactyloides [Nutt.] Engelm. Ligule: a fringe of hairs, 0.5–1 mm long.
  • Mowing: 1.0–2.0 inches. Fertilization: 0.5–2.0 lb. N / K ft² / year. Irrigation: minimal or none. Other: no cultural practices needed.
  • Golf Turf — Greens: not adapted. Tees: not well adapted. Fairways, Roughs: adapted. Athletic: not well adapted. Lawn: adaptable to low cultural intensities. Utility: well adapted in drier climate.
  • A native American short-prairie-grass species. Ploidy varies geographically: Mexican/Gulf Coast populations diploid (2N=2X=20), most of Mexico tetraploid (2N=4X=40), most of the U.S. hexaploid (2N=6X=60) — evolving from diploid in Mexico as it migrated north; higher ploidy (more allele versions per locus: 2/4/6) means broader adaptation.
  • Dioecious: male (staminate) flowers on elevated slender culms; female (pistillate) flowers form a burr hidden within the leaves. In El Paso, forms an acceptable lawn with far less water than other warm-season turfgrasses need.
Source: M14.6_ Buffalograss_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Textbook enrichment — Ch. 3, pp. 74–77, 83

  • Chloridoideae: warm-season, C4 (Hatch and Slack) grasses needing warm nights and no vernalization; Pooid in spikelet traits but Panicoid in chromosome/embryo/root/stem/leaf traits. Three of its five tribes carry turfgrass genera: Cynodonteae (major genera Cynodon and Bouteloua), Zoysieae (sole genus Zoysia), and Andropogoneae (Eremochloa/centipedegrass, outside this module).
  • Common bermudagrass has three races (tropical: short, loose turf; temperate: winter hardy, denser; seleucidus: cold tolerant, coarse, thick stolons/rhizomes), may occur as far north as 53°N, spreads by both stolons and rhizomes, and has folded vernation. Early U.S. greens used African seed; surviving-clone selection gave U-3; Tiflawn was an early intraspecific hybrid; recurrent selection later refined cultivars.
  • African bermudagrass (cultivar Uganda) parents many fine hybrid cultivars. Magennis bermudagrass (cultivar Sunturf) and hybrid bermudagrass are both sterile triploids with 27 chromosomes and folded vernation.
  • Hybrid bermudagrass cultivar lineage: Tiffine (1953, too stemmy for greens) → Tifgreen/Tifton 328 (1956, the greens standard) → Tifway/Tifton 419 (a chance 1954 hybrid from an African seed packet, released 1960, popular on fairways/sports fields for its stiff, erect growth) → Tifdwarf (a 1962 natural mutant in an old Tifgreen green, released 1965, shorter leaves/internodes) → ultradwarfs FloraDwarf (1995) and TifEagle (cobalt-induced from Tifway II, 1997), plus somatic-mutation cultivars Champion, Classic, Florida Dwarf, MS Supreme, MiniVerde, and Reesegrass. Ultradwarfs are largely restricted below 32°N latitude; cold-hardier Latitude 36, Northbridge, Patriot, and Riviera serve sports fields/lawns farther north, where creeping bentgrass takes over for greens.
  • Gramagrasses (Bouteloua, ~50 species) include buffalograss, blue grama, and sideoats grama. The textbook classifies buffalograss as Bouteloua dactyloides — differing from the module page's traditional genus, Buchloe — with base chromosome number 10, diploid/tetraploid/hexaploid clones, rolled vernation, and a preference for fine-textured alkaline soils. Blue grama (tetraploid, 40 chromosomes) has folded vernation; sideoats grama, less drought tolerant, instead has rolled vernation and long adaxial hairs.
  • Of 11 Zoysia species, three are turfgrasses, differentiated by aggressiveness, texture, and cold tolerance. Japanese lawngrass tolerates cold enough to persist (impractically) even in subarctic climates, discolors near 50–55°F into a straw-colored winter turf, needs heavy reel-type mowers for its tough shoots, and thrives in transitional climates; cultivar Meyer was historically dominant, with newer Crowne/El Toro/Palisades (vegetative) and Compatibility/Zenith (seeded). Manilagrass is intermediate in texture/cold tolerance (cultivars include Zorro); mascarenegrass is the finest, densest, slowest-growing, and least cold tolerant, usually kept as unmowed ground cover, where heavy thatch causes surface convolutions.
  • Per the leaf-anatomy section (p. 83): bermudagrasses have folded vernation with smooth, sometimes hairy leaves; zoysiagrasses all have rolled vernation, with some species showing sparse long hairs confined to the adaxial surface.

Textbook enrichment — Ch. 3, pp. 78–82

  • Panicoideae: short- or intermediate-day C4 (Hatch and Slack) grasses needing warm nights and no vernalization; one-flowered, dorsally compressed spikelets that disarticulate below the glumes. Of 12 tribes, Paniceae (Axonopus, Paspalum, Pennisetum, Stenotaphrum) and Andropogoneae (Eremochloa; paired sessile + pediceled spikelets) carry turfgrass genera.
  • Bahiagrass: open, erect, low-density turf, highly susceptible to weed invasion. Var. saurae (small-seeded sexual diploid, 20 chromosomes; cultivar Pensacola, Florida roadsides) vs. var. latiflorum (large-seeded apomictic tetraploid, 40; cultivar Argentine, lawns). Deep roots, very drought tolerant; iron deficiency above pH 7.5; submersion tolerant but not salt tolerant; not very shade tolerant; rotary-mow off summer seed heads; highly susceptible to triazines (atrazine, simazine) and sulfometuron.
  • Seashore paspalum (sand knotgrass): native to southern Africa and South America; dense, fine-textured, dark green; diploid, 20 chromosomes; marshy/brackish and saline soils, tolerates waterlogging and flooding; more shade tolerant than bermudagrass. Mowed 0.25–0.5 in generally, down to 0.125 in for Sea Isle 2000 (first greens cultivar). Scalping recovery very slow — light frequent topdressing, limited N and irrigation, adequate K, gradual height reduction. Vertical mowing controls puffiness.
  • Kikuyugrass: Pennisetum (~80 species); invades subtropical and highland tropical turfs. Medium-textured, light green, tough, dense, springy turf; tetraploid, 36 chromosomes. Moist, medium-textured, high-fertility soils; thrives above 6000 ft in the moist tropics of Mexico and central Africa but severely diseased in warm damp lowlands; grows 60–90°F and keeps more color below 60°F than most warm-season grasses; serious weed in coastal southern California. Textbook mowing 0.5–2 in with a heavy reel mower, 1–3 lb N; trinexapac-ethyl reduces scalping proneness.
  • St. Augustinegrass: one of six Stenotaphrum species; compressible, spongy canopy; prostrate leaves resist weed invasion; interbreeds with pembagrass (S. dimidiatum). Moist, well-drained, sandy, slightly acid soils; poor cold tolerance (mild coastal areas); chinch bugs. Races: Breviflorus (diploid, 18 — Gulf Coast Group, green stolons, cold tolerant: Texas Common, Raleigh; Dwarf Group, red stolons; hybrids Del Mar, Jade, Seville are shade tolerant), Longicaudatus (open, low-culture: Florida Common, Roselawn), Cape (polyploid, sterile, grayish-green: Bitterblue; Floratam/FX-10 resist some chinch bugs and SAD).
  • Centipedegrass: one of 10 Eremochloa species; diploid, 18 chromosomes; moist, acid, sandy, low-fertility soils; chlorosis above pH 7.5; deep-rooted, fairly drought tolerant. Cultivars: Oaklawn (failed — poor seed production), AU Centennial (compact), Tenn Turf (cold tolerant), Tifblair (seeded, cold tolerant). Leaf anatomy: St. Augustinegrass and centipedegrass flat, smooth, folded; bahiagrass smooth, flat, rolled.

Overview objectives: In this Module you will review information on Climatology. · Identify components of weather influencing turfgrass quality. · Determine factors that cause these weather components to change from site to site. · Conclude how shade influences the quality and cultural requirements of turfgrasses.

Overview

  • Short distances change conditions dramatically: sun to tree shade, windy to sheltered side of a building, concrete to vegetation. Water bodies and cities also change the weather.
  • Chicago-area July/January averages: Waukegan, Lake Michigan north shore, 71.9/22.5°F; Marengo, 40 mi inland, 72.7/20.5°F; City of Chicago 74.7/24.3°F (warmest — heat island); Argonne, 30 mi west of Chicago, 72.9/22.2°F.
Source: M16.1_ Overview_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Climatology & Topography

  • Climatology = study of the atmospheric component of the environment. Measurable conditions: temperature, moisture, light, wind.
  • Daily changes: earth's rotation on its axis. Seasonal changes: revolution around the sun, shifting the equator relative to insolation (incoming solar radiation). Spring/fall: insolation most direct at the equator; winter: below it; summer: above it. Poleward: harsher winters, milder summers; toward the equator: hotter summers, milder winters.
  • Large water bodies moderate diurnal and seasonal swings — oceanic sites are milder in summer and winter than continental sites. Average temperatures fall appreciably with relatively small increases in altitude.
Source: M16.2_ Climatology_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf; M16.3_ Topography_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Light

  • Leaves absorb ~50 to nearly 80% of incident radiation (horizontal leaves absorb best: 0.94 vs. 0.85 vs. 0.62 vertical); only 1–2% becomes chemical energy. Most is reradiated at longer wavelengths as heat; some is reflected or transmitted.
  • Shade (clouds, trees, buildings): moderate shade makes leaves more upright ("reaching up"); extreme low light — turf fails to persist. Reduced light: thinner, longer leaves; less density and tillering; shallower roots; thinner cuticles; lower carbohydrate reserves.
  • PAR = red (600–700 nm) + blue (400–500 nm). Net photosynthesis = photosynthesis − respiration (µmol/m²/s); curvilinear response to PAR. Light compensation point: net photosynthesis = 0 (below it plants cannot survive). Light saturation point: ~1/3 full sun for cool-season grasses, much higher for warm-season. Hot weather → photorespiration in cool-season grasses.
  • Morning shade (trees east of a green) is more damaging than afternoon shade: morning brings recovered moisture status (guttation), cool temperatures, low photorespiration, and high net photosynthesis. Moderate afternoon shade (trees to the west) can help by reducing moisture stress and photorespiration.
  • Tree canopies lower the red:far-red ratio. Phytochrome: Pr (blue, inactive) absorbs red and is converted to Pfr by red light; Pfr (olive-green, active) absorbs far-red and reverts to Pr by far-red light or darkness. Active form: inhibits elongation, promotes tillering (smaller, denser plants). Inactive form: promotes elongation, inhibits tillering. (The module page labels both headings "Pr"; the active form is Pfr.)
  • Light exclusion = ultimate shading problem (e.g., heavy clippings left on turf); injury within hours in high heat. Photoperiod: short days early and late in the season and long days mid-season at intermediate latitudes; short days give denser, more tillered, shorter-leaved, more prostrate plants (8 h vs. 16 h figure).
Source: M16.4_ Light_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Temperature

  • Temperature is the most important factor in a turfgrass's regional adaptation. Heat transfer: evaporation, radiation, conduction, convection, advection. "Heat island" turfs are stressed as adjacent areas absorb and re-radiate heat. Chicago north shore: cooler summers and warmer winters than inland; the city is warmer in both.
  • Cardinal temperatures: maximum effective, minimum effective, and optimum (growth range); plus maximum and minimum survival. Daily high at midday, low just before sunrise; bare soil surface fluctuates more than air at 5 ft (weather-service height).
  • Shade effects — quantitative: reduced PAR; qualitative: phytochrome photomorphogenesis. Tree-shaded turf has thinner, longer, more upright leaves, thinner cuticles, shallower roots, and lower carbohydrate reserves, so it is more delicate and less tolerant of wear, disease, and stress.
Source: M16.5_ Temperature_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Ice Damage & Species x Hardening

  • Causes: gas-related problems (anoxia, hypoxia, toxic butanol/ethyl butyrate), crown hydration (ice crystals in crowns where standing water freezes after a rapid early-spring temperature drop on poorly drained sites), and ice encasement (rapid freezing in intercellular spaces). Loss of cold-hardy proteins also contributes.
  • Photos: creeping bentgrass green survived; perennial ryegrass fairway died. Annual bluegrass (brown) died; creeping bentgrass (green) survived. Better surface drainage dramatically improves survival. Ice broken up mechanically then refrozen killed an entire green — remove ice if possible, otherwise leave it undisturbed.
  • Ice survival depends on species (creeping bentgrass tolerant, annual bluegrass intolerant) and fall hardening — hardening can be just as decisive. Under ice, plants respire crown TNC (total nonstructural carbohydrate) anaerobically. Hardened bentgrass ~87% survival vs. ~17% annual bluegrass; unhardened ~18% vs. ~11%.
  • Hardened crowns: 30 d 7.8% TNC/55%, 60 d 4.8%/53%, 90 d 4.5%/49% (r² = 0.73). Unhardened: 30 d 5.5%/31%, 60 d 4.4%/10%, 90 d 3.8%/1% (r² = 0.97). Averaged across species and air injection treatments.
Source: M16.6_ Ice Damage_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf; M16.7_ Species x Hardening Interaction_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Moisture

  • Actively growing turf is ~90% water. Water functions: cell turgidity, transport of nutrients and organic compounds, protoplasm, raw material (e.g., photosynthesis), and temperature buffering through transpiration.
  • Transpiration = absorption, transport, and release of water; evaporation takes ~570 cal/g, cooling the plant. Vapor exits mainly via stomates and forms a boundary layer (up to 1 cm with rapid transpiration, high RH, no wind; a few mm otherwise).
  • Leaf folding/rolling = drought avoidance. Moderate deficits → wilting (temporary stomatal closure). Severe, prolonged wilting can kill turf — especially under high cultural intensity or heavy traffic — or cause dormancy.
  • Dew: large droplets = guttation through hydathodes at leaf tips (root water pressure); small droplets = condensation when radiation cooling drops leaf temperature and adjacent air reaches its dew point.
Source: M16.8_ Moisture_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Wind

  • Wind = air in motion; acts through mixing action and debris transport. Differential heating starts global circulation: equatorial air rises and flows poleward aloft; descending air at 30° latitude and the poles forms highs (clockwise in the northern hemisphere, counterclockwise in the southern). Lows form where air masses converge at intermediate latitudes, bringing frontal weather; their circulation is opposite to highs.
  • Wind disperses boundary layers, accelerating transpiration in direct proportion to velocity. Mixing redistributes temperature, humidity, CO₂, and dust. Blocked air (dense trees and shrubs) creates a hot, humid, disease-prone microclimate. Severe prolonged winds cause desiccation on exposed sites.
  • Snow cover insulates and prevents desiccation. Slow freezing → black (clear) ice: hydration rises, crystals form in crowns. Slush/freezing rain → white (cloudy) ice, much more damaging: rapid freezing pulls moisture from tissues. Winter cover: permeable plastic, then 6–12 in straw, then impermeable plastic. Solid-tine cultivator removes ice in thin 1/2-inch layers.
Source: M16.9_ Wind_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Textbook enrichment — Ch. 4, pp. 93–111

  • Environment = atmospheric, edaphic, and biotic dimensions (together with the turf community, the turfgrass ecosystem). Glossy or wet leaves reflect more light.
  • Shade response likely involves gibberellic acid (trinexapac-ethyl helps shaded turf). Shade management: raise mowing height, reduce N, fertilize trees separately (holes on 2-ft centers), irrigate infrequently and deeply, use trinexapac-ethyl, fungicides as needed, root-prune and limb trees, remove fallen leaves. HPS grow lights (Manchester stadium, 600 W); continuous 24-h light can harm turf.
  • Evaporation converts sensible to latent heat. Advection heats small turf strips next to pavement; it can also cool turf near water. Importance ranking: radiation, convection, transpiration, wind (managers control transpiration and wind). Heat acclimation → acquired thermotolerance. Cold acclimation (hardening off) raises nonstructural carbohydrates and lowers hydration; undermined by excess N, low K, excess irrigation, close mowing, thatch, and poor drainage. Thermoperiodism. South-facing slopes favor bermudagrass.
  • Drought escape (annual bluegrass seed production), avoidance, and tolerance; leaf firing; abscisic acid. Warming saturated air from 62 to 87°F doubles its vapor content. Exudation from cut leaves; guttation fluids can feed pathogens and burn tips. Dew is removed by poling, dragging, or syringing. Annual bluegrass dies after ~75 days of ice cover; creeping bentgrass survives at least 120. Excelsior mats last 3 years; K raises osmotic potential.
  • NC State fans: canopy 4–10°F cooler, 4-inch soil 2–6°F cooler, top-6-inch soil moisture 5–10% lower. U.S. air masses move west to east; Canadian highs are dry and cold. Sea/land breezes, Santa Ana, and chinook winds. Blocked air favors brown patch, Pythium, and Helminthosporium diseases; windbreaks reduce desiccation; wind carries pollutants (SO₂, fluorides, ozone).
  • Heat transfer detail: reradiation at longer (infrared) wavelengths; conduction through soil particles (air is a poor conductor); convection plumes; advective cooling near water. Survival temperatures depend on exposure period. Thermotolerance: heat shock proteins; inducible by salicylic acid, abscisic acid, ethylene, calcium; 18:2 → 18:3 linoleic acid desaturation increases membrane fluidity. Cold acclimation: lipid desaturation lowers membrane crystallization temperature; sugars restrict ice growth in cell walls and raise vacuole osmotic pressure; abscisic acid rises; antifreeze proteins bind ice crystals.
  • Soil surface is hotter than air by day, slightly cooler at night; turf stays cooler than artificial surfaces. Mulching bermudagrass before winter aids survival. Topography makes latitude/altitude predictions unreliable; the south side of buildings is hottest. Shade light is rich in far-red (700–800 nm); tree roots may exude toxic chemicals; shade moderates temperature, restricts air, raises RH. Spectrum-adjusted HPS adds blue light.
  • Boundary-layer math at 86°F: 40% RH → 12.74 vs. 31.85 mm Hg, gradient 19.11; 80% RH → gradient 6.37. Annual bluegrass seed has high-temperature-enforced summer dormancy. Endophytes (Neotyphodium, Epichloe) may protect against pests causing water loss. Oceans cover ~3/4 of the earth; condensation nuclei form clouds; condensation releases latent heat. Dew forms on clear nights; preventive fungicides go on before snowfall; covers go on after the last fall mowing.
  • Lows form where two highs meet; Pacific moisture falls west of the Rockies; mountain/valley winds go upslope by day and downslope at night; Santa Ana = drainage wind. Soil profile: O (organic residue), A (topsoil), B (subsoil, accumulated leachates), C (partially weathered parent material), R (bedrock); soil takes thousands of years to form; three phases: solid, liquid, air.
Source: Turfgrass Management Edition 1.0, Chapter 4, pp. 93–111
